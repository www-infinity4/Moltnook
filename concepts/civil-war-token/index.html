1863 Civil War Token E Pluribus Unum

A merchant-issued store card token from Staudinger's, 116 Broadway, New York City — struck during the Great Coin Famine of the American Civil War, when private copper replaced the vanished government cent and kept everyday commerce alive.

🔍 Token Design 📜 The Coin Famine
🪙 Numismatics · History

Money born of necessity.

When the U.S. government's copper cents disappeared from circulation in 1862, thousands of merchants printed their own — and one of the most eloquent bore the republic's founding motto.

The 1863 Staudinger's store card is a Civil War token — a category of privately minted emergency coinage that circulated across the United States from roughly 1861 to 1864. Roughly the size and weight of the contemporary U.S. one-cent piece, these tokens served as functional substitutes when government-issued cents were hoarded out of existence.

What makes the Staudinger's piece particularly striking is its obverse legend: E PLURIBUS UNUM — Latin for "Out of Many, One." The republic's national motto, born to describe the union of thirteen colonies, here takes on a second resonance: out of many private issuers, one workable currency system. Necessity reinvented patriotic symbolism into pragmatic monetary policy.

The reverse carries the merchant's imprint: STAUDINGER'S · 116 · BROADWAY · N.Y. — a commercial address in lower Manhattan that served as both advertisement and guarantee. The token was redeemable at that specific address, anchoring abstract money to a real counter on a real street.

Numismatic Anatomy

The two faces.

Every token tells a story on both sides — patriotism on the obverse, commerce on the reverse.

O
Obverse

🦅 The Patriotic Face

The obverse typically features an eagle or shield motif — standard patriotic imagery borrowed from official U.S. coinage — encircled by the legend E PLURIBUS UNUM. Issuers adopted federal symbols deliberately to confer legitimacy on their private issue, reassuring holders that this copper disc was as trustworthy as government coin.

R
Reverse

🏪 The Merchant Face

The reverse bears the merchant's address: STAUDINGER'S · 116 · BROADWAY · N.Y. Broadway at that era was already one of the most commercially dense streets in the United States. The street number was the token's guarantee — lose it and you knew exactly where to redeem it.

E
Rarity

📊 Mostly R — Rarity Scale

Civil War token rarity is measured on the Fuld Rarity Scale, running from R1 (very common, thousands surviving) to R10 (unique). A rating of Mostly R — shorthand for a borderline R-category — indicates that survivorship is meaningful but not trivial: enough examples exist for study, yet each specimen carries real scarcity value.

📜 American History · 1861–1864

The Great Coin Famine.

A war, a metal shortage, and a nation of merchants who refused to let commerce stop.

When the Civil War broke out in 1861, financial panic swept the United States. Citizens hoarded gold and silver coins — then copper cents. By the summer of 1862, virtually no small change circulated in the North. Merchants were forced to make change with postage stamps, privately printed scrip, and IOUs. Commerce ground to an awkward, irritating halt at the counter of every shop on every street.

Into this void stepped private die-sinkers — craftsmen who already cut advertising dies and medal stamps. Working with cent-weight copper planchets, they struck millions of small tokens bearing merchant names, patriotic slogans, and political imagery. The Mint was aware and broadly tolerated it; the tokens were functionally identical in size and weight to the legal cent, and they kept the economy moving.

Congress finally addressed the shortage in April 1864 with the Coinage Act of 1864, which authorized the bronze Indian Head cent and made it a criminal offense to issue or use private copper coins as currency. Almost overnight, Civil War tokens were retired — but not before an estimated 25 million pieces had been struck by hundreds of private issuers across the country.

Timeline

From shortage to suppression.

1861

War Begins — Hoarding Starts

Fort Sumter falls in April 1861. Financial panic spreads. Silver and gold disappear from circulation as citizens hoard metal. The copper cent follows within months.

1862

Postage Currency & Stamp Money

The Treasury authorises Postage Currency — small paper notes bearing stamp images — as a stopgap. Merchants also accept actual postage stamps glued into small cardboard holders. The system is chaotic and inconvenient.

1863

Peak Token Production

Die-sinkers in New York, Philadelphia, and Cincinnati strike tokens for hundreds of merchants, including Staudinger's of 116 Broadway. Estimated production this year alone exceeds 15 million pieces. The E Pluribus Unum store card is among thousands of distinct types catalogued by collectors today.

1864

Coinage Act — Tokens Outlawed

The Coinage Act of 1864 mandates the new bronze one-cent piece and criminalizes private cent-substitutes. Civil War tokens are demonetised. Many are melted; survivors become the numismatic treasures catalogued in the Fuld reference used by collectors today.

Historical note. Civil War tokens are collected under the broader category of exonumia — objects that resemble coins but were never issued as official government currency. The Fuld reference system, developed by George and Melvin Fuld in the twentieth century, catalogues over 10,000 distinct Civil War token die combinations. This page documents the Staudinger's E Pluribus Unum store card as a primary example of New York merchant-issue emergency coinage.

Why It Matters

Money as a mirror.

What does a copper cent tell us about resilience, improvisation, and the nature of trust?

🤝

Trust at the Counter

A store card token worked because both parties agreed it worked. The merchant's address printed on the reverse was the contract — a promise to redeem, backed by physical location. It is perhaps the most honest form of money ever issued: a liability openly declared.

🗽

E Pluribus Unum Reborn

The motto "Out of Many, One" gained a second meaning on Civil War tokens. Thousands of individual merchants, each issuing their own copper, collectively formed one functional small-change system — bottom-up monetary policy driven by necessity rather than legislation.

📡

A Signal Across Time

Every surviving token is a direct physical signal from 1863. The copper has not changed; the die marks are unchanged. Holding one is holding the exact object that passed between a New York shopkeeper and a customer during the most fractured period in American history.
